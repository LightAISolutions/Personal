### How cycle life converts into money

The cycle-life row above is the one asset in the table that a candidate can state and still fail to sell, because "13,000 cycles" is a spec and specs do not move buying decisions. **What moves them is a number in the owner's financial model.** Cycle life produces two, and you should be able to name both. Mizhi Zhang ran the side of the table that consumes these numbers, so this is the one place in the conversation where speaking the buyer's language will be unmistakable.

**The mechanism: a flat obligation against a decaying asset.** A project sells its output on a fixed promise — 100 MW for four hours, every day, for twenty years. That promise does not decay; the cells do, from both cycling and calendar aging. LFP typically retains **70–80% of original capacity after ten years** of daily cycling, and supplier warranties conventionally guarantee **70% retained at year ten** or at a stated number of equivalent full cycles. So the owner has exactly two ways to keep meeting a flat obligation:

1. **Oversize on day one** — installing **15–20% more capacity than the obligation requires is standard practice**. You pay up front for cells you will not need for a decade
2. **Augment later** — install near nameplate, then add racks or containers in tranches (typically years six to ten, and again later) to restore rated capacity. You defer the cost, and you accept a retrofit on a live, energized site

Most models blend the two, and either way **lifetime capex is a function of the warranted degradation curve** — which is what cycle life proxies for. Lazard-style methodology carries augmentation at roughly **3% of ESS equipment cost per year** as a standing reserve, so this is not a rounding error in the model. A better curve pushes the first tranche out by years and can eliminate one inside the contract term; deferral alone is worth real money once discounted at the owner's cost of capital. **That is "augmentation capex avoided" — a dated, quantified line the customer's analyst already has in a spreadsheet, and which your cell moves.**

**Two traps, both of which he will know:**

- **A cycle count is not a degradation curve.** "≥13,000 cycles" is a rating to a retention threshold, measured at a stated temperature, depth of discharge and C-rate. What a model actually consumes is the **year-by-year guaranteed capacity table** plus the conditions it was measured under — a big headline number taken at a gentle 0.25C and 25 °C delivers nothing on a project that runs harder and hotter. The sharp industry formulation: a vendor who will not show guaranteed end-of-year capacity out to year fifteen is hiding the augmentation cost in the *developer's* future rather than its own. **Ask for that table before the customer does**
- **Cycles beyond what the duty cycle consumes are worth zero.** 13,000 cycles at one per day is ~35 years; on a twenty-year daily-cycling project everything past ~7,300 is free and useless. This is why the 314 Ah's 13,000 is *not* automatically better than the 587 Ah's 11,000. The headroom converts to money only under **high-throughput duty** — frequency regulation, multiple cycles a day, aggressive merchant dispatch. Which makes **"what's your expected annual throughput — cycles per year, not cycles total?"** a real qualifying question, because the answer tells you whether cycle life is your lead argument for that account or an irrelevance

**The second number, and the harder one: the warranty reserve.** This cuts both ways. On the **seller's** side, a multi-year performance warranty requires booking a warranty provision — an estimated future liability — at the time of sale, as a charge against gross margin; a better cell means provisioning less per MWh sold. On the **buyer's** side, a warranty is a promise from a counterparty, so lenders and independent engineers will **discount or entirely disregard** warranty value from a supplier they judge unlikely to survive the term, then require mitigants — parent guarantee, letter of credit, escrowed funds, third-party warranty insurance — each of which costs money and appears as a line item.

**Why the two halves are really one argument.** Hithium's cell is genuinely strong on the first number. But Hithium is private, has failed to list three times, and never published FY2025 financials — so an independent engineer can haircut the warranty **regardless of how good the cell is**, at which point the degradation advantage never reaches the model, because the model will not credit a promise it does not believe. **The cell is the product; the warranty's credibility is the constraint on selling it.** That is why the objection table pairs cycle life with the warranty-backstop question, and why asking him whether there is an insurance wrapper or only a balance sheet is on the question list.

**The speakable version:**

> "I'd sell cycle life as two numbers in their model rather than as a spec. First, augmentation — their obligation is flat and the asset decays, so they're either oversizing on day one or buying tranches around years six and eleven, and Lazard-style models carry that at roughly three percent of equipment cost a year. A better warranted curve moves those out and can drop one entirely. Second, the warranty reserve — and I'd be honest that this one isn't about the cell. An IE will haircut a warranty from a private supplier that hasn't published a full year of financials, and if they do, my degradation advantage never reaches the model. So what we can put behind the warranty is the thing standing between a good cell and a financeable one."

**One connection worth raising as a question, not a claim.** Augmentation and the FEOC regime intersect: cells added years after a project is placed in service are new equipment bought under whatever rules apply then, so a build safe-harboured in 2025 may not get the same treatment for a 2032 tranche. If that holds, it makes the cycle-life advantage *more* valuable than it looks — fewer augmentation events means fewer future compliance exposures for the owner. **This is my inference, not a sourced ruling** (commentary notes augmentation can jeopardize ITC treatment; nothing found rules on augmentation under material assistance specifically). Raise it as a question for his tax advisers and it lands as sophistication; assert it as fact and it is exactly the overclaim this brief warns against.

## The regulatory stack — three rules, three different exposures

This is the section to know cold. Three separate US actions hit this company differently, and most candidates will blur them into "tariffs and China stuff." Keeping them distinct — and knowing which one actually bites — is the strongest single thing you can demonstrate.

### 1. FEOC / Prohibited Foreign Entity rules — the existential one

Enacted 4 July 2025 in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act; effective for facilities **beginning construction in 2026 or later**; Treasury/IRS guidance in **Notice 2026-15** (February 2026).

| Mechanism | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Material Assistance Cost Ratio (MACR)** | (A − B) ÷ A, where A is direct cost of all project equipment and B is the direct cost of equipment supplied by prohibited foreign entities. Storage projects starting construction in **2026 must clear ≥55% non-PFE**, rising to **≥75% for 2030+ starts** |
| **The penalty** | Failing the ratio forfeits **the entire 30–40% ITC** — not a haircut, the whole credit |
| **Why storage is the hardest case** | Cells are the largest single cost line in a BESS project and are overwhelmingly Chinese. DOE's supply-chain work found 90–100% of US BESS systems contain at least one Chinese-sourced component, and ~65% of global Tier-1 BESS vendors are Chinese-headquartered |
| **The trap** | **US assembly does not automatically cure it.** Trade press in February 2026 ran the point explicitly — "Made in the USA" batteries might still fail FEOC. Where the box was screwed together matters less than who supplied the value inside it |

**How to talk about it:** as the central commercial fact of the role, and as a question rather than a solved problem.

> "The way I read the market since January is that it split into two tiers — FEOC-compliant supply now carries a premium and unlocks the domestic-content adder, and non-compliant supply is cheaper but can cost the developer the whole credit. That inverts the usual pitch: I wouldn't be selling on price, I'd be selling on whether a project can be financed. Which is why I'd want to understand where Mesquite actually lands in a material-assistance calculation today, and what the plan is for cell production on US soil — because that's the difference between competing for the whole market and competing for the slice that isn't chasing the ITC."

**Where to hedge, hard.** I could not find a source that definitively rules on how a Chinese-controlled manufacturer's US-assembled product is treated in the MACR, and the answer likely turns on facility-specific facts and on structure. **Say "my read", never "the rule says."** If pushed, the correct answer is that this is a question for tax counsel and the developer's tax-equity investor, not for a salesperson — and that answer is a strength, not a dodge. The industry's emerging workaround is **structural**: joint ventures and licensing arrangements in which a non-prohibited US entity owns the production (the NeoVolta / Potis Edge / Longi arrangement in Georgia is the example the trade press cites). Note the bind that creates for Hithium — **its Reliance Industries technology-licensing deal collapsed in January 2026 because Beijing tightened export controls on battery technology transfer.** So the very structure that would solve the Washington problem is currently constrained from the Beijing end. If you can say that sentence cleanly, you will be the most informed candidate he sees this month.

#### Beijing's half of the squeeze

The Reliance collapse is worth understanding properly, because it is the mechanism that makes the FEOC problem structurally hard rather than merely expensive.

- **What Beijing did.** On **15 July 2025** MOFCOM and the Ministry of Science & Technology amended the *Catalogue of Technologies Subject to Export Prohibitions and Restrictions*, adding **LFP and LMFP cathode preparation technology** (plus lithium carbonate/hydroxide and gallium processing) — and simultaneously **lowered the technical thresholds** (compaction pressure 300 → 220 MPa, iron-phosphate tap density 2.1 → 1.2 g/cc), widening the net to catch newer LFP processes. On **9 October 2025** MOFCOM and Customs escalated with Announcement No. 58, effective **8 November 2025**, extending dual-use controls to lithium batteries, cathode materials and graphite anodes
- **The mechanism, precisely.** The technology is **restricted, not prohibited** — it sits under licence management and can be transferred *with* MOFCOM authorization. Nobody banned the Reliance licence; Beijing converted it from a commercial decision into a discretionary permission. Note the sequencing: rules tightened in July, escalated in November, and Hithium withdrew in **January 2026** — which reads as a company concluding the licence would not be granted, not one that received a refusal
- **It is a pattern, not an incident.** **Amara Raja's licensing deal with Gotion ended the same way** — its executive director: *"All technical collaboration has stopped"* — and the company now expects Chinese tech curbs to raise Indian cell prices. Exide's SVOLT licence, signed in 2022, survives; new agreements are not being written. Even surviving arrangements are throttled by visa denials for Chinese engineers
- **The bind, stated plainly.** *Washington* says the value has to come from a non-prohibited entity if the project wants the credit — so localize, license, restructure. *Beijing* says the cathode process is restricted technology requiring its permission to transfer. **The structural workaround to the American rule is the exact transfer the Chinese rule has made discretionary.**
- **Which is why the answer here is wholly-owned plants.** Mesquite and the €400M Navarre cell factory are localization that does not hand technology to a third party. The export-control regime pushes Hithium toward precisely the strategy it is running

> **Two cautions.** **(1)** Reliance **publicly and categorically denied** pausing its battery plans and said its 2026 timeline was unchanged — and it owns Lithium Werks (LFP) and Faradion (sodium-ion), so it has fallbacks. Say *"the licensing route stalled under Chinese export controls,"* never *"Reliance halted its gigafactory,"* or you will be contradicted by someone who was in the room. **(2)** Do not open this as *"what happened with Reliance?"* — that invites him to hear a probe about a failure. Frame it forward, as a question about how the export-control regime shapes localization.

### 3. The FCC covered-list action — the one that mostly misses them

On **28 July 2026** the FCC added **foreign-produced connected power inverters** (and advanced robotic devices) to its Covered List, effective immediately. Covered-List products cannot receive FCC equipment authorization, which bars importing, marketing or selling them in the US.

**It is a two-part test — both prongs must be met:**

| Prong | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Conversion** | A **bi-directional** device or system converting DC→AC or AC→DC, enumerated as microinverters, string inverters, central inverters, and **hybrid (battery-based) inverters** |
| **Connectivity** | Contains components enabling remote communication, control, sensing, data collection or monitoring via **Wi-Fi, cellular, Bluetooth or other similar connections** |

Three things almost everyone gets wrong: **it is not a China rule** — "foreign-produced" means failing the Buy America threshold of 65% US content by value, so an American brand manufacturing offshore is caught too; **it is prospective only** — models authorized before 28 July keep their authorization and can still be produced, imported and sold; and **there is a conditional-approval path** out.

**Where Hithium actually sits:**

| Product line | Exposure | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Utility DC blocks** (∞Block, ∞Power, Flexsso) | **Probably out** | These ship as **DC** blocks — the buyer or a partner supplies the PCS. No inverter in the scope of supply means the conversion prong is not met on a plain reading |
| **Residential and portable** (HeroEE LIGHT 1, HeroEE MaxPower 8 AIO, ARKVOLT L16/L16S) | **Genuinely exposed** | These are hybrid/battery-based units with integrated inverters and MPPT, and consumer products carry wireless connectivity as standard. Both prongs plausibly met — but this is a small adjacency, not the core business |
| **Cells** | **Out** | Not conversion devices |

**The observation that will land — and it is about his old company:**

**And notice the asymmetry, but do not gloat about it.** FEOC hurts Hithium considerably more than the FCC action does. The FCC action hurts inverter and PCS vendors — the business Mizhi Zhang came from — considerably more than it hurts Hithium. Stating that cleanly and neutrally shows range. Stating it with a smile does not.

## The hard questions

The winning move is the same every time: **name the real difficulty, then show you understand why the company chose what it chose.** Do not be a cheerleader. He knows these numbers better than you ever will, and a candidate who flinches from the hard version of the story is a candidate who will flinch in front of a customer.

**"What's your read on our position in the US market right now?"** — The honest structure: the product is strong and the policy is against it. Top-2 global scale, a certification portfolio that clears US code, a plant in Texas shipping since August 2025, and prospectus customers like Jupiter Power and Lightsource bp — that is not a company with an awareness problem. What changed on 1 January is that Chinese cell content stopped being a cost advantage and started being a financing risk, because a developer failing the material-assistance ratio loses the whole ITC. So the constraint isn't demand or credibility; it's that the buying decision moved from procurement to tax counsel. Then the sharp bit: almost every flagship win in the last eighteen months is outside North America, which reads either as deliberate sequencing toward duration-mandating markets or as the US being genuinely hard right now — and you'd want to know which he thinks it is.

**"Tesla will be manufacturing Megapack domestically from late 2026. How would you sell against that?"** — Do not pretend it isn't a problem. A US-manufactured integrated system clears the FEOC math without any structural work; that is a real, durable advantage on ITC-driven projects. Three responses. **Segment away from the collision**: safe-harboured and pre-2026 construction-start projects are outside material assistance entirely, and augmentation and expansion of existing fleets is a large ITC-safe pool. **Compete where they don't**: nothing in Tesla's catalogue is 8-hour-native, and there is no kAh-class alternative. **Then be honest** that the medium-term answer isn't a sales tactic, it's US cell production or a compliant structure — which is a strategy question for him, not a pitch from you.

**"We're private and we've failed to list three times. Does that worry you?"** — Answer as a seller, not an investor. It worries *customers*, which makes it your problem: a twenty-year warranty is only as good as the entity standing behind it, and lenders and insurers price that. So the useful question isn't whether the listings failed, it's what the company can put in front of a buyer instead — shipment scale, cash generation (H1 2025 operating cash flow above RMB 1B), and whatever warranty-backstop or insurance structure exists. Then the analytical point: three failed attempts against a capex-heavy expansion means **financing capacity, not demand, is the binding constraint** — and Spain and any US cell capacity are exactly what a listing was meant to fund. So the financing problem and the FEOC problem are the same problem seen from two ends.

**"What worries you about us?"** — A real answer, calmly delivered, is a strength. Three candidates. **Concentration of the hard news in one place**: FY2025 financials were never published, so the most recent full picture available to a customer's credit team is FY2024 — where profit depended on RMB 414M of subsidies. **Merchant-channel fragility**: Powin was a cell customer and went bankrupt. **The licensing bind**: the structural fix for FEOC is a compliant local production arrangement, and the Reliance deal showed that Chinese export controls now constrain exactly that. Frame all three as things you'd want to understand, not accusations.

**"You sent us something about the FCC inverter rule. What's your actual read?"** — Assume this may come up, since you emailed it to Rumman Kabir on 12 August. Go a level deeper than the headline: it is a **two-prong** test — a bi-directional inverter *and* wireless connectivity — and it is an **origin** test rather than a China test, because "foreign-produced" means failing Buy America's 65% US-content threshold. Then the Hithium-specific read: DC blocks ship without a PCS, so the conversion prong isn't met on a plain reading; the residential line with integrated inverters is where it actually bites; and it is prospective only, so previously authorized models are fine. Close on the second-order effect — our customers' PCS options just narrowed, so it's worth bringing to developers proactively. **And label it as your read**, because no source addresses DC-block supply directly.

**"How would you open a US developer or integrator account for us, cold?"** — The wrong answer is a pitch; the right answer is a sequence. Qualify on **compliance posture before product fit**: has this project safe-harboured, when does construction start, is it chasing the ITC at all? That single question sorts your pipeline into winnable and unwinnable faster than anything else. Then find the person who owns the tax position, not just the engineer — because that is where the deal dies. Lead with the specs that convert into money (cycle life as augmentation capex avoided). Make the first ask small: one tranche, one block, a limited qualification. And use Mesquite early — a plant a customer can drive to answers "can you support us here" with a place rather than a promise.

**"What would your first ninety days look like?"** — Concrete and modest. Segment the pipeline by compliance posture rather than by geography, because that is now the real axis. Learn exactly what Mesquite can and cannot claim in a material-assistance calculation, since that bounds every promise you make. Find out which prospectus relationships — Jupiter Power, Lightsource bp, Samsung C&T — are live versus historical. Build a relationship with one tax-equity-side adviser, because you will need a translator. Then say the honest thing: with qualification cycles this long, ninety days produces qualified pipeline and a compliance playbook, not revenue — and you'd want to agree upfront which leading indicators you'll both judge it by.

## Questions worth asking them

With a business president the bar is the **business**, not the company. Pick three or four; do not run the list.

**The one that will be remembered**

> *"You built Sungrow's North American business before this. What did you learn there about getting a Chinese-origin product qualified by American buyers that you're applying here — and how much of that playbook still works now that FEOC exists?"*

It is warm rather than adversarial, it assumes competence, it is specific to him in a way no generic candidate could manage, and whatever he says is the single most useful thing you could possibly learn before day one. If you ask only one question, ask this.

**About the compliance crux**

1. **"Where does Mesquite actually land in a material-assistance calculation today — and is US cell production on the roadmap or off it?"** — The commercial heart of the role. His answer tells you whether you'd be selling into the ITC market or around it.
2. **"How do we answer the twenty-year warranty question for a lender — is there a backstop or insurance structure, or is it balance-sheet only?"** — Sophisticated, and it is the objection you'd hear most.
3. **"Does the Chinese export-control regime on cathode technology shape how we localize — is that part of why the answer is wholly-owned plants rather than licensed local production, and does it touch the Navarre cell plant at all?"** — This is the deep one. It shows you understand the bind runs in both directions, and the Navarre half is a genuine open question: if cathode preparation technology is restricted for export, it is not obvious that transferring it to your own overseas subsidiary escapes the regime. **Ask it; do not assert it** — nothing published addresses intra-group transfers, so as a claim it is an overclaim and as a question it is the most interesting thing you'll say.

**About the territory**

3. **"Almost every flagship win in the last eighteen months is outside North America while the plant is in Texas. Is that deliberate sequencing, or is the US just hard right now?"** — Shows you read the record rather than the summary, and invites an honest answer.
4. **"Do we lead with cells to integrators or with our own DC blocks — and when those two collide inside one account, who wins?"** — The channel-conflict question. He crossed that exact line personally, so he will have a real view.
5. **"The 1300 amp-hour cell and the 6.9 megawatt-hour system land in Q4. Is North America an early market for eight-hour duration, or is that Australia and the Gulf first?"** — Defines what you'd actually be selling next year.

**About the desk**

6. **"What does applications and pre-sales engineering look like in-region — and does Mesquite do customer validation, or only assembly?"** — In a technical qualification sale this is the difference between a workable territory and an impossible one.
7. **"How is the desk structured — do I own accounts end to end across both the cell and system businesses, or is it split?"** — Practical, and it shows you know the dual model has a seam in it.

**The bold one — use only if the conversation is going well**

8. **"Tesla starts building Megapack domestically late this year. How do you think about competing for ITC-driven projects against a US-manufactured integrated product?"** — Highest risk, highest reward. It names the real threat rather than the obvious one. Ask it as genuine curiosity about strategy, never as a challenge, and only once you've earned the standing to.

## Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **LFP** | Lithium iron phosphate — the storage-standard cathode chemistry: cheaper, safer and longer-cycling than nickel chemistries, at lower energy density. Fine for stationary storage, where weight barely matters |
| **Prismatic** | A cell in a rigid rectangular can (versus cylindrical or pouch). Packs efficiently into modules; the format all of Hithium's large cells use |
| **kAh-class** | A cell rated at 1,000 amp-hours or more. Hithium's 1175 Ah was the first in mass production, and no rival has matched it |
| **NFPP** | Sodium iron pyrophosphate — the cathode in Hithium's sodium-ion cell |
| **DC block vs AC block** | A **DC block** is batteries + BMS + thermal management in a container, delivering DC — the buyer supplies the PCS. An **AC block** includes the PCS and delivers grid-ready AC. Hithium sells DC blocks; Sungrow and Tesla sell AC-side integrated systems. **This distinction drives Hithium's FCC exposure and its channel strategy** |
| **PCS** | Power conversion system — the bi-directional inverter between the DC battery and the AC grid |
| **EMS / BMS** | Energy management system (dispatch and market logic) / battery management system (cell-level protection and balancing) |
| **RTE** | Round-trip efficiency — energy out ÷ energy in. ~95% DC is current best practice; every lost point is revenue |
| **Cycle life** | Charge/discharge cycles to a defined capacity retention. 13,000 cycles at one cycle a day is over 35 years — which is why cycle life converts into *augmentation* rather than lifespan |
| **Augmentation** | Adding cells later to restore a degraded system's rated capacity. A capex line in the project model, and the thing high cycle life eliminates |
| **Capacity guarantee** | The supplier's warranted retention schedule — conventionally 70% of original capacity at year ten, or at a stated number of equivalent full cycles. **The year-by-year table, not the headline cycle count, is what a financial model consumes** |
| **Warranty provision / reserve** | On the seller's books, the accrued estimated liability for honouring future warranty claims — a charge against gross margin. On the buyer's side, the discount a lender or independent engineer applies to a warranty whose counterparty they doubt, plus the cost of any mitigant (parent guarantee, letter of credit, escrow, warranty insurance) required to close the gap |
| **DoD / C-rate** | Depth of discharge (how far a cell is emptied) / charge-discharge rate relative to capacity. A 4-hour system runs 0.25C |
| **LDES / duration** | Long-duration energy storage. "Duration" is hours at rated power — 2h, 4h, 8h. Some jurisdictions now *mandate* it (NSW: 28 GWh by 2033) |
| **ITC / 48E** | The federal investment tax credit for clean-energy property, 30–40% of eligible cost — the economics of most US storage projects |
| **45X** | The production tax credit for US-made components: ~US$35/kWh for cells, ~US$10/kWh for modules |
| **Domestic content adder** | A 10% bonus on the ITC for meeting US-content thresholds |
| **FEOC / PFE** | Foreign entity of concern / prohibited foreign entity — the OBBBA regime restricting tax-credit eligibility for supply chains tied to China, Russia, Iran and North Korea |
| **MACR** | Material assistance cost ratio — (A − B) ÷ A, the share of project equipment cost *not* from prohibited entities. Storage needs ≥55% for 2026 construction starts, ≥75% from 2030 |
| **Safe harbour / beginning of construction** | Tax rules fixing which year's requirements apply. Projects that began construction in 2025 or earlier are **outside** material assistance — which makes them Hithium's cleanest addressable market |
| **Section 301 / Section 122** | The China-specific tariff programme (non-EV lithium-ion went 7.5% → 25% on 1 Jan 2026) / the flat 10% surcharge in the live stack after the Supreme Court struck the IEEPA tariffs in February 2026 |
| **UL 9540 / UL 9540A / UL 1973 / NFPA 855** | The US safety and code stack: system certification / thermal-runaway fire-propagation test / battery-module standard / the installation fire code. Missing any of these is a hard stop in US procurement |
| **Bankability** | Whether lenders, insurers and independent engineers accept a vendor. Third-party determined, slow to earn, and the real gate on a private Chinese supplier |
| **Tax equity / IE** | The investor monetizing the tax credits (and the party whose counsel can veto your supply) / independent engineer, the technical reviewer signing off for lenders |
| **IPP / EPC** | Independent power producer (owns and operates the project) / engineering, procurement and construction contractor (builds it) |
